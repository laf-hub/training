Why allergens matter

For someone with a food allergy, even a tiny trace of the wrong ingredient can trigger a reaction ranging from mild to anaphylaxis, which can be fatal. Getting allergen information wrong is one of the most serious mistakes a food business can make, and one of the most preventable.

Core ruleWhen in doubt, check. Never guess.

Allergy, intolerance & coeliac disease aren't the same

  • Allergy: an immune-system reaction; can be severe or life-threatening, and can be triggered by tiny/trace amounts.
  • Intolerance: the body struggles to process something (e.g. lactose); unpleasant (bloating, discomfort) but not immune-driven and not usually life-threatening.
  • Coeliac disease: an autoimmune condition triggered by gluten; not an allergy, but requires very strict avoidance of cross-contact, similar in practice to a severe allergy.

Take every customer request seriously regardless of which of these it is, but understanding the difference helps you understand why the level of care needed can vary.

Quick check

A customer says they're "a bit lactose intolerant" and asks if a small taste of a milk-based sauce would be fine. Should you treat this the same as a severe nut allergy question? (click to reveal)

Take it just as seriously as a request, but the underlying risk differs: intolerance is unpleasant, not usually life-threatening. Answer accurately either way, check rather than guess, but don't assume every question implies the same level of medical emergency risk.

The 14 major allergens

UK/EU law requires these to be identified whenever used as ingredients:

  • Celery
  • Cereals containing gluten
  • Crustaceans
  • Eggs
  • Fish
  • Lupin
  • Milk
  • Molluscs
  • Mustard
  • Nuts (tree nuts)
  • Peanuts
  • Sesame
  • Soya
  • Sulphur dioxide/sulphites (above 10mg/kg or per litre)

Worked scenario: a customer asks about an allergen

A customer asks, "does the walnut cheese contain any other nuts?" Here's the response, step by step:

  1. Don't answer from memory or assumption, even if you're fairly confident. "Fairly confident" isn't good enough when the consequence of being wrong is severe.
  2. Check the allergen matrix for that specific product, not a similar-sounding one.
  3. If the matrix doesn't cover it clearly, or you're still unsure, check with the kitchen or a supervisor before answering, even if it takes a minute longer and the customer is waiting.
  4. Give a clear, specific answer: which allergens are present, rather than a vague reassurance like "it should be fine."
  5. If you genuinely can't confirm it, say so honestly and offer an alternative, rather than guessing to avoid an awkward pause.

Recipe changes and supplier substitutions

One of the most common real-world causes of allergen incidents isn't careless handling, it's a product that was safe last month and quietly isn't any more. A chutney recipe gets tweaked to include mustard seed. A supplier changes their recipe for a coating or seasoning without it being obvious from the product name. A cheese starts being finished with a nut-based crust that a previous batch didn't have.

None of these changes are visible on the shelf, and none of them are your fault when they happen, but they become your problem the moment a customer asks and you answer based on what used to be true. Any recipe change or supplier substitution has to trigger an allergen matrix update before that product is sold again, not "whenever someone gets round to it."

Why an outdated allergen matrix is dangerous

An allergen matrix is only as safe as its last update. A matrix that's technically "on the wall" but hasn't been checked in months creates a false sense of security: staff trust it, follow it exactly, and give a confident, correct-sounding answer that's actually wrong, which is arguably worse than an honest "let me check."

  • Review the matrix on a set schedule, not just when someone remembers.
  • Update it immediately after any recipe change, supplier change, or new product, before that product is sold again.
  • Make sure every member of staff who might be asked an allergen question knows where the current version is, and that it's the current version.

Quick check

You know a product's allergen matrix hasn't been reviewed in months, but you're confident nothing's changed. Do you answer from it anyway? (click to reveal)

Answer honestly about the uncertainty rather than presenting an unreviewed matrix as current fact. Flag it to a supervisor so it gets reviewed and updated, since "probably still accurate" isn't good enough for an allergy question.

Natasha's Law

Since October 2021, Natasha's Law requires full ingredient and allergen labelling on Prepacked for Direct Sale (PPDS) food: food packed on the same premises it's sold from before the customer orders it (e.g. a sandwich made in-house and put in the chiller). It followed the death of Natasha Ednan-Laperouse from a severe allergic reaction to sesame in a baguette that wasn't labelled with its allergens.

"May contain" advisory labelling

You'll often see wording like "may contain traces of nuts" on packaging. This is a voluntary precautionary warning used when there's a real risk of accidental cross-contact during manufacturing (e.g. shared production lines). It is not the same as the mandatory 14-allergen list, and it isn't something to use loosely as a get-out. If a customer asks and you're not sure whether "may contain" wording applies to something made in-house, treat it the same as any other allergen question: check, don't guess.

Quick check

A customer with a severe nut allergy asks whether a "may contain nuts" label means the product definitely doesn't contain nuts as an ingredient. What do you tell them? (click to reveal)

"May contain" is a precautionary cross-contact warning, separate from the mandatory 14-allergen ingredient list. It doesn't mean nuts are a listed ingredient, but it does mean there's a real risk of trace contact. Explain both clearly rather than treating the label as a simple yes/no.

Preventing cross-contact

  • Use separate utensils, chopping boards, and prep areas for allergen-free orders where possible.
  • Wash hands and change gloves between handling allergenic and allergen-free food.
  • Store allergen ingredients separately, clearly labelled, and below allergen-free food.
  • Clean surfaces and equipment thoroughly between different allergen orders.

Talking to customers

Always take an allergy question seriously. If you don't know the answer, check with the kitchen/supplier before answering, never guess. Keep an up-to-date allergen matrix for your menu, easily accessible to all staff, and update it whenever a recipe or supplier changes.

Emergency response: if a customer becomes unwell

If a customer shows signs of a serious allergic reaction, swelling of the face, lips or throat, difficulty breathing, dizziness, or collapse, this is a medical emergency, not a customer service situation:

  1. Call 999 immediately if symptoms are severe or breathing is affected. Don't wait to see if it passes, and don't let anyone delay the call to "manage the situation" first.
  2. Ask if they carry an adrenaline auto-injector (e.g. an EpiPen) and help them use it if they're unable to, following the device's instructions, if you're trained to do so.
  3. Keep them calm and, if possible, seated or lying down while help is on the way. Don't leave them alone.
  4. Gather what they ate: the product, its packaging or allergen information, and anything else consumed, so paramedics and the customer have accurate information.
  5. Tell a supervisor immediately, even while the emergency is ongoing if someone else can do it.
  6. Record the incident fully afterwards: what was eaten, what was said before, what symptoms appeared and when, and what action was taken. This record matters for the customer's ongoing care and for reviewing what went wrong in your process.

Summary

  • 14 major allergens must be identified whenever used.
  • Recipe changes and supplier substitutions are a leading real-world cause of allergen incidents; update the matrix immediately, not eventually.
  • Natasha's Law requires full labelling on PPDS food.
  • Never guess on allergen questions; check and confirm, every time.
  • A severe reaction is a 999 emergency: act immediately, then record fully afterwards.

Because it's an autoimmune condition triggered by gluten, and even small amounts of cross-contact can cause harm, so in practice it needs the same level of care as an allergy, even though the underlying mechanism is different.
