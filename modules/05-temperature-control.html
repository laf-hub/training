Why temperature control matters

Temperature is the single biggest lever you have over bacterial growth. Keeping food out of the 8–63°C danger zone (Module 3), except for the minimum time needed for preparation, is central to safe food handling. Every target temperature in this module exists to answer one question: does this keep food out of the range where bacteria multiply fastest, or move it through that range as quickly as possible?

Core ruleTime in the danger zone is the risk you control.

Chilling

Legal maximum for chilled food is 8°C, but best practice, and what most businesses target, is 0–5°C, giving a safety margin for door-opening and loading. Check and log fridge temperatures regularly.

Quick check

A fridge reads 9°C. What should you do first? (click to reveal)

Don't just note it and carry on. Check the obvious causes (door seal, overstocking, whether it's been left open), move high-risk food to a working fridge if needed, and report the fault so it gets fixed, not just logged and forgotten.

Worked example: a delivery arrives too warm

A chilled delivery arrives and the probe reads 10°C on the outer packaging, above the 8°C legal maximum. What do you actually do?

  • Check properly first: probe more than one item, and check the core temperature of a representative item, not just the surface, since surface temperature can be misleadingly high after time on a warm vehicle.
  • Use judgement within your training: a small, brief excursion might be within your supplier's agreed tolerance; a high reading on a high-risk item usually isn't. If you're unsure, don't guess, ask a supervisor before accepting.
  • Reject if in doubt: it's far cheaper to reject one delivery than to serve unsafe food. Keep the item aside, don't put it into general storage while you decide.
  • Record it either way: temperature, time, item, and what you did (accepted, rejected, or escalated). This is a HACCP delivery check (Module 7), not an optional note.

Worked example: fridge temperature rising

You check the fridge log and notice it's reading 9°C, above the legal maximum, having been fine yesterday.

  • Don't just note it and move on. Check the door seal, whether it's overstocked (blocking airflow), and whether it's been left open recently.
  • Move high-risk food to a working fridge if the fault can't be fixed immediately, rather than leaving it to keep drifting warmer.
  • Use the 2-hour/4-hour rule (covered shortly) to judge whether food that's been above temperature is still usable, based on how long it's actually been out of range, not a guess.
  • Report the fault so the unit can be repaired or replaced, and log the incident, the readings, and what was done with the affected food.

A fridge quietly running warm for days because "it's probably fine" is exactly the kind of gradual failure that a good temperature-logging habit is designed to catch early.

Freezing

Freezers should run at -18°C or below. Freezing stops bacterial growth but doesn't kill bacteria already present. They can become active again once food thaws, so thawed food must still be handled and cooked as if freshly prepared.

Thawing (defrosting) safely

Thawing at room temperature is one of the most common real-world causes of food poisoning: the outside of the food sits in the danger zone for hours while the inside is still frozen.

  • Thaw in a fridge (0–5°C), in a container that catches drip/run-off away from other food, allowing enough time (large items can take 24–48 hours).
  • Alternatively use a microwave's defrost setting if the food will be cooked immediately afterwards.
  • Never thaw at room temperature on a worktop, and never re-freeze raw food once thawed unless it's cooked first.

Cooking

Cook food to a core temperature of at least 75°C (or 70°C held for 2 minutes) to destroy harmful bacteria. Always check the thickest part of the food, away from bone, using a clean, calibrated probe thermometer.

Hot-holding & the 2-hour/4-hour rule

Food held hot for service (e.g. a carvery, bain-marie, or bar food display) must stay at 63°C or above. If it drops below that, UK guidance uses a two-tier rule based on total time out of temperature control:

  • Under 2 hours: can still be returned to the fridge or back to hot-holding.
  • 2–4 hours: can still be served immediately, but must not be returned to the fridge or hot-holding again.
  • Over 4 hours: must be thrown away.

Each of these clocks only runs once per item; you can't reset it by briefly reheating or rechilling.

Worked example: a hot-holding failure

Mid-service, you notice the bain-marie has been reading 58°C for a while, below the 63°C minimum, but you're not sure exactly how long.

  • Work out the actual time out of temperature as best you can, using the last confirmed good reading, not just "it looked fine an hour ago." If you genuinely don't know, treat it as the more cautious estimate rather than the optimistic one.
  • Apply the 2-hour/4-hour rule to that estimated time to decide whether food can still be served, must be used immediately and not returned, or must be discarded.
  • Fix the equipment fault before refilling: check it's switched on, the setting is correct, and it isn't overloaded.
  • Record the incident: what happened, your estimated time out of temperature, and what you did with the affected food. This is exactly the kind of corrective action your HACCP process (Module 7) expects to see logged, not just handled quietly.

Cooling: why shallow containers cool faster

The chain of cause and effect is worth spelling out in full: food comes out of the oven and starts to cool → as it drops below 63°C it enters the danger zone → any surviving bacteria and spores (Module 3) begin multiplying again → the longer it takes to cool, the more time they have.

A large, deep container of hot food cools slowly because heat can only escape from its outer surface, and the centre of a deep pot stays hot (and in the danger zone) for a long time even in a cold fridge. Splitting the same food into smaller, shallower containers dramatically increases the surface area it can lose heat from relative to its volume, which is what actually makes it cool faster, not just "the fridge is cold so it'll be fine eventually."

Worked example: cooling a large batch

You've cooked a large pot of soup or stew for service. To cool it safely within 90 minutes:

  • Divide it into several smaller, shallower containers rather than one deep pot, for the reason on the previous slide.
  • Leave lids off or ajar until the food has cooled, to let steam and heat escape, then cover once cool.
  • Use an ice-water bath or blast chiller if available; stir occasionally to bring warmer food from the centre to the cooler edges.
  • Don't put a large, still-hot container straight into a packed fridge: it will raise the temperature of everything around it and may push other food into the danger zone too.
  • Once cooled, label with the date and move it into normal chilled storage.

Reheating

Reheat food to at least 75°C, and only reheat food once. Each reheat cycle gives surviving bacteria/spores another chance to multiply during cooling and re-storage; repeated reheating multiplies that risk. Remember from Module 3: reheating kills live bacteria, but it can't remove a heat-stable toxin that's already formed from food that was mishandled earlier.

Quick check

A tray of reheated stew wasn't finished during service. Can it be cooled, stored, and reheated again tomorrow? (click to reveal)

No. Food should only be reheated once. Each additional reheat cycle gives any surviving bacteria or spores another chance to multiply during the cooling and storage in between, so a second reheat compounds the risk rather than being "extra safe."

Using a probe thermometer

  • Clean and disinfect the probe before and after each use (probe wipes) to avoid cross-contamination between foods.
  • Insert into the thickest part of the food, avoiding bone and packaging.
  • Check calibration periodically (e.g. in melting ice, should read close to 0°C).
  • Record temperature checks; this evidence supports your HACCP records (Module 7).

What to record, and when to escalate

Temperature control leaves a paper trail almost everywhere: delivery checks, fridge/freezer logs, cooking and reheating checks, cooling records, and any corrective action. The pattern is always the same:

  • Record the reading, not just "checked, fine" or "checked, bad", the actual number, the time, and the item.
  • Record what you did about it if a reading was out of range: rejected, moved, discarded, escalated.
  • Escalate to a supervisor whenever you're unsure, whenever equipment seems to be faulty rather than a one-off reading, or whenever food may already have been served before you spotted the problem.

These records aren't paperwork for its own sake. They're what lets you (and an EHO, Module 1) show that a problem was caught and controlled, rather than ignored.
