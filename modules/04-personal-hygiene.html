Personal Hygiene

Duration: 11–14 minutes

What this module covers

  • Why personal hygiene matters, and why handwashing actually works
  • Handwashing: when and how
  • Protective clothing, jewellery, nails and cuts
  • A realistic scenario: how a lapse turns into contamination
  • Illness reporting and exclusion periods
  • Rules for food areas
  • What to record when illness or a hygiene lapse happens

Why it matters

People are one of the biggest sources of food contamination, through unwashed hands, illness, hair, and unclean clothing. Good personal hygiene is one of the simplest and most effective controls you have, because unlike temperature control or cleaning schedules, it depends entirely on individual habit, done correctly every single time, not just when someone's watching.

Why handwashing actually works

Soap doesn't kill bacteria on contact the way a disinfectant does. What it does is break down the oils on your skin that bacteria and grease stick to, so that friction and running water can physically lift them off and rinse them away. That's why the scrubbing time and coverage (palms, backs, between fingers, thumbs, wrists) matter as much as the soap itself: a quick rinse under the tap does almost none of this.

Alcohol hand gel kills many bacteria on contact but doesn't remove dirt, grease, or organic matter, so it's a poor substitute when hands are visibly soiled or have handled raw food. It's a supplement to proper washing, not a replacement for it.

Handwashing: when

  • Before starting work and after breaks.
  • After using the toilet.
  • After handling raw food, waste, or cleaning chemicals.
  • After touching your face, hair, or phone.
  • Before handling ready-to-eat food.
  • After handling money (retail/bar).

Handwashing: how

Wet hands, apply soap, and scrub for at least 20 seconds: palms, backs of hands, between fingers, fingertips, thumbs, and wrists. Rinse thoroughly and dry with a single-use towel or air dryer, not on your apron. Use a dedicated hand-wash basin, not a food-prep sink, since a food-prep sink is meant to stay as clean as the food that goes in it.

Protective clothing

  • Clean uniform/apron, changed regularly and when visibly dirty.
  • Hair tied back and/or covered.
  • No outdoor clothing worn over food-prep clothing.
  • Remove or cover protective clothing before using the toilet.

Jewellery, nails & cuts

  • Minimal jewellery: typically a plain wedding band only; no rings with stones, bracelets, or dangling earrings near open food.
  • Nails short, clean, and free of nail polish or false nails.
  • Cover cuts and grazes with a blue, metal-detectable plaster, plus a glove where appropriate.

What can go wrong: a realistic scenario

You've just cashed up a round of card and cash payments at the till. A colleague asks for help plating a ready-to-eat cheese board, so you step over and start arranging slices with your hands. No gloves, no wash in between, because it felt like a thirty-second favour, not a "handling food" moment.

Money is handled by dozens of people and surfaces before it reaches you, and hands are the single most common route for indirect contamination (Module 2). This kind of lapse doesn't feel like a mistake in the moment, which is exactly why it's the one that catches people out: the risk isn't in the big, obvious tasks, it's in the quick ones that don't feel like they count.

Illness reporting

Report to your supervisor before a shift if you have diarrhoea, vomiting, a fever, or an infected wound. Anyone with diarrhoea or vomiting should not handle food and must stay away from food areas until symptom-free for 48 hours: this is the standard exclusion period used to prevent spreading illnesses like norovirus (Module 3).

This 48-hour rule covers most everyday cases, but it isn't absolute: if a doctor confirms a more serious cause (e.g. E. coli O157 or typhoid), public health authorities can require a longer exclusion and formal clearance before you return to food handling. Always follow what your supervisor and any medical guidance tell you, rather than treating 48 hours as a fixed rule that overrides everything else.

In food areas

No smoking, eating, drinking (other than water from a covered container), or chewing gum in food preparation, storage, or bar-service areas. These all create routes for contamination, from hand-to-mouth contact to items being set down on food-contact surfaces.

What to record and report

  • Illness reported before a shift should be logged by your supervisor: name, symptoms, date reported, and the date they're cleared to return.
  • If you realise after the fact that you handled ready-to-eat food without washing your hands properly (like the till-to-cheese-board scenario), tell your supervisor. It's a small correction in the moment and a much bigger problem if it's only discovered later.
  • A cut covered mid-shift, especially one that bled near food or a surface, should be reported so the area can be checked and cleaned if needed, not just covered and left.

Summary

  • Handwashing works through friction and rinsing, not soap alone. Alcohol gel supplements it, but doesn't replace it.
  • Wash hands thoroughly and at every key point in the day, including quick or "favour" tasks, not just planned food prep.
  • Keep hair, nails, jewellery, and clothing in check.
  • Report illness before your shift; the 48-hour rule applies to sickness/diarrhoea.
  • Report hygiene lapses and mid-shift cuts, don't just quietly correct and move on.
