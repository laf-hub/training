Module 9 of 10

Kitchen-Specific Practice

Everything so far applies wherever food is handled. This module focuses on practices specific to back-of-house kitchen work.

Duration: 10–13 minutes

What this module covers

  • Goods-in checks, including vacuum-packed products and traceability
  • Separating raw and ready-to-eat food
  • Probing every batch, not just the first item
  • Storage, stock rotation, and waste management

Goods-in checks

Kitchen Check every delivery: temperature (chilled ≤8°C, frozen ≤-18°C on arrival), use-by dates, packaging integrity, and signs of pest activity or damage. Reject and record anything that fails; don't put it into storage "to sort out later."
Core ruleCheck every batch, not just the first.

Vacuum-packed products and traceability

Kitchen A vacuum-packed item that's blown (the film puffed up rather than tight to the product) is a red flag, it can mean gas-producing bacterial spoilage inside a pack that looked fine from the outside a moment ago. Reject blown or leaking packs on sight, and check for this specifically, not just for the delivery temperature.

Note the batch code and traceability information from the delivery label for anything you goods-in, not just the use-by date. If a product is ever the subject of a recall or a customer complaint, that code is what lets you check whether the affected batch is the one you received, rather than guessing.

Raw vs ready-to-eat separation

Kitchen Use separate prep areas, boards, and utensils for raw and ready-to-eat food wherever the kitchen layout allows. Where space is tight, always prep ready-to-eat food first, before raw meat/fish touches the same surface, and fully clean and disinfect between the two.

Quick check

Kitchen space is tight and only one board is available. You need to prep both raw chicken and a ready-to-eat salad. What order do you do it in? (click to reveal)

Ready-to-eat first, while the board is clean, then raw chicken. Doing it the other way round means the salad would need the board fully cleaned and disinfected first anyway, so prepping ready-to-eat before raw avoids that step entirely.

Probing in a working kitchen

Kitchen Probe every batch of high-risk food, not just "the first one out." Keep probe wipes at every station where probing happens, and log checks at busy service points (e.g. Sunday roast, batch-cooked curries). These are exactly the situations where corners get cut under pressure.

Storage & stock rotation

Kitchen Follow FIFO (First In, First Out): newer stock goes behind or under older stock so older items get used first. Store raw meat/fish on the lowest shelves of the fridge, in sealed containers, below ready-to-eat and cooked food.

Quick check

A new delivery arrives while there's still older stock of the same item on the shelf. Where does the new stock go? (click to reveal)

Behind or under the older stock, so the older items are still the first to be used. Putting new stock at the front, even briefly "for now," is exactly how FIFO breaks down and items go past their date unused at the back.

Kitchen waste management

Kitchen Use foot-operated, lidded bins in prep areas. Separate food waste for collection where required. Empty bins regularly during service, not just at close; overflowing bins attract pests and are a physical/chemical contamination risk.

Summary

  • Check every delivery before it goes into storage, including vacuum-seal integrity, and note batch/traceability codes.
  • Separate raw and ready-to-eat food by space and time.
  • Probe every batch, not just the first item.
  • FIFO stock rotation and prompt waste removal reduce risk.

1. A vacuum-packed product arrives with the film puffed up rather than tight to the product. What should you do? (click to reveal)

Reject it. A blown pack can be a sign of bacterial spoilage inside, even if the product looks fine and the temperature is correct.

2. Why is it worth noting the batch code on a delivery, not just the use-by date? (click to reveal)

If a product is ever the subject of a recall or a customer complaint, the batch code is what lets you check whether the specific batch you received and used is the affected one, rather than guessing across everything with a similar date.

3. Why probe every batch of a busy Sunday roast rather than just the first tray out of the oven? (click to reveal)

Different batches, or even different items within a busy service, can cook unevenly. A single check on the first tray tells you nothing about whether a later one reached a safe core temperature, especially under the time pressure that causes corners to get cut.

4. Cumulative: a customer reports feeling unwell several days after eating with you. Why might the cause not be obvious, and what records would help investigate? (click to reveal)

Incubation periods (Module 3) for causes like Campylobacter or E. coli can run 2–5 days, so there's often no obvious link back to a specific meal by the time symptoms start. Delivery, batch, and traceability records (this module and Module 7) let a supervisor check what was received, prepared, and sold on the relevant date, turning a vague complaint into something that can actually be investigated.
