Why this matters

Poor food handling causes real harm: food poisoning outbreaks, allergic reactions, and in the worst cases, death. It also carries serious consequences for a business: prosecution, fines, forced closure, and lasting reputational damage.

This course gives you the knowledge expected of anyone handling, preparing, or serving food or drink, whether in a kitchen, behind a bar, or on a retail counter.

Key terms

  • Food safety: ensuring food is fit and safe to eat.
  • Hazard: anything with the potential to cause harm (a microbe, a shard of glass, a nut in a "nut-free" meal).
  • Risk: the likelihood that a hazard will actually cause harm.
  • Contamination: the presence of something harmful or undesirable in food that wasn't originally there.
  • Food-borne illness: sickness caused by consuming contaminated food or drink.

The Food Safety Act 1990

The core UK law governing food safety. It makes it an offence to:

  • Sell food that is unfit for human consumption, or not of the nature/quality/substance demanded.
  • Sell or advertise food in a way that falsely describes it or misleads as to its nature.
  • Handle food in unhygienic conditions or ways that render it injurious to health.

It gives Environmental Health Officers (EHOs) legal power to inspect premises, seize unsafe food, and prosecute.

Food hygiene regulations

Sitting under the 1990 Act, the Food Hygiene (England) Regulations 2006 (and equivalents in Scotland, Wales, Northern Ireland), alongside retained Regulation (EC) 852/2004, set out day-to-day operational requirements:

  • Food business operators must identify hazards and put controls in place. This is where HACCP comes in (Module 7).
  • Premises, equipment, and staff must meet defined hygiene standards.
  • Records of food safety practices must be kept and made available to inspectors.

Quick check

An EHO arrives unannounced during a busy Saturday lunch service. What should happen? (click to reveal)

Staff carry on cooperating and let them inspect; EHOs are legally allowed to inspect without notice. Nobody should refuse entry, delay them, or scramble to "tidy up" practice that isn't normally followed. The right response is to keep working safely and answer their questions honestly.

Who enforces it

Environmental Health Officers (EHOs), employed by local authorities, inspect food premises (typically unannounced), rate them under the Food Hygiene Rating Scheme (0–5 in England, Wales & NI; Scotland uses a separate pass/improvement-required scheme), and can:

Displaying the rating sticker is a legal requirement in Wales and Northern Ireland, but currently only voluntary in England, so check which applies to your premises.

  • Issue improvement notices.
  • Issue a Hygiene Emergency Prohibition Notice to close a premises immediately if there's an imminent risk to health.
  • Prosecute, leading to unlimited fines and/or imprisonment for serious breaches.

The Food Standards Agency (FSA) sets national policy and guidance that EHOs work to.

What can go wrong: a realistic scenario

An EHO visits unannounced on a normal Tuesday. The food itself is handled fine, nothing is out of temperature, nothing is contaminated, but when they ask to see your cleaning schedule and temperature logs, there's nothing written down for the past two weeks, just "we do it, we just don't always log it."

This is a genuinely common way premises end up with an improvement notice: not because food was actually unsafe on the day, but because there was no evidence to show the EHO that safe practice is happening consistently, not just when someone remembers. Good practice that isn't recorded is, from an inspection point of view, indistinguishable from practice that isn't happening at all.

Whose responsibility is it?

Employers must provide adequate training, supervision, and facilities (handwashing, refrigeration, cleaning equipment) for food to be handled safely.

Employees have a personal legal duty too, to follow the food safety practices they've been trained in, report illness, and not do anything that puts food safety at risk. Legislation applies to you, not just your employer.

Quick check

A new colleague says "food safety is the manager's problem, not mine." Are they right? (click to reveal)

No. Employees carry a personal legal duty under the Food Safety Act 1990 too, to follow the practices they've been trained in, report illness, and not do anything that puts food safety at risk. The law applies to individual staff, not only the business.

What to record, and why it protects you

Every later module in this course comes back to the same habit: temperature checks, cleaning schedules, delivery checks, illness reports, corrective actions, all written down, not just done. This isn't paperwork for its own sake.

Records are what let you (and your employer) demonstrate due diligence, that reasonable steps were taken to prevent a food safety problem, which is a genuine legal defence under the Food Safety Act 1990. Without records, a business handling food correctly and a business that got lucky can look identical to an inspector. With records, you can actually show which one you are.

Summary

  • Food law exists to prevent harm and hold businesses and individuals accountable.
  • The Food Safety Act 1990 and the 2006 Regulations (plus 852/2004) are the key legal framework.
  • EHOs enforce the law locally; the FSA sets national policy.
  • An inspection can find a business at fault for missing records, even when the food itself was handled safely on the day.
  • Both employer and employee carry legal responsibility, and good records are your evidence of due diligence.

1. Which piece of legislation makes it an offence to sell food that is unfit for human consumption? (click to reveal)

The Food Safety Act 1990.

2. Why does the law require records, not just correct practice? (click to reveal)

Because correct practice that isn't recorded can't be told apart from luck, from an inspector's point of view. Records are what prove reasonable steps were taken consistently, the legal defence of "due diligence."

3. An EHO finds your food handling is fine, but your cleaning log hasn't been filled in for two weeks. Could you still get an improvement notice? (click to reveal)

Yes. Missing records are a genuine finding on their own, even when the food itself was handled safely on the day, because there's no evidence the good practice is consistent rather than a one-off.

4. Who is legally responsible for food safety in a business: the employer, or the employee? (click to reveal)

Both. Employers must provide training, supervision, and facilities; employees have a personal legal duty to follow that training, report illness, and not put food safety at risk.
