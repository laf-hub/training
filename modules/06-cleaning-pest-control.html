Cleaning, disinfecting, sanitising

  • Cleaning: physically removes dirt, grease and food debris (detergent + water).
  • Disinfecting: reduces micro-organisms to a safe level (chemical disinfectant, correct contact time).
  • Sanitising: combines both in one product/step, common on food-contact surfaces.

Cleaning alone doesn't kill bacteria; disinfection is a separate, necessary step for food-contact surfaces.

Core ruleA quick wipe is not a clean.

Why contact time and dilution matter

A disinfectant doesn't work the instant it touches a surface. It needs contact time, a set number of minutes (check the product label, often 1–5 minutes) during which the surface has to stay visibly wet with the chemical. Spray-and-wipe-immediately doesn't give it time to actually work; it just spreads the chemical around without killing much.

Dilution matters just as much. Too weak, mixed with too much water, and the chemical doesn't reach the concentration it needs to be effective. Too strong doesn't mean "extra safe": it wastes product, can leave harmful residue on food-contact surfaces, and some chemicals are actually less effective outside their designed concentration. Always follow the manufacturer's dilution instructions exactly, not an estimate.

Why dirty surfaces reduce disinfectant effectiveness

Grease, food debris, and organic matter physically shield bacteria underneath them from a disinfectant, and can chemically react with and weaken some disinfectants before they ever reach the germs. Disinfecting a visibly dirty surface gives a false sense of safety: it can look and smell "disinfected" while bacteria survive underneath the residue you didn't clean off first.

This is exactly why cleaning always comes before disinfecting, never the other way round or combined into a single wipe on a dirty surface. Clean first to physically remove the debris, then disinfect the now-clean surface so the chemical can actually reach and kill what's left.

Quick check

You're about to serve the next customer, so you spray disinfectant on the counter and wipe it off straight away. Is the surface now disinfected? (click to reveal)

No. Disinfectant needs contact time, staying visibly wet on the surface for the minutes stated on the label, to actually work. Wiping it off immediately means it hasn't had time to kill anything.

The six stages of cleaning

  1. Pre-clean: remove loose debris.
  2. Main clean: detergent loosens grease/dirt.
  3. Rinse: removes detergent and loosened dirt.
  4. Disinfect: kills remaining micro-organisms (with correct contact time).
  5. Final rinse: removes disinfectant residue (where required).
  6. Dry: ideally air-dry; damp surfaces encourage bacterial growth.

Worked example: cleaning a chopping board between tasks

You've just finished trimming raw chicken on a board and need it ready for ready-to-eat vegetables next. Step by step:

  1. Pre-clean: scrape off any visible meat scraps into waste.
  2. Main clean: wash with hot water and detergent, scrubbing the whole surface, including cut grooves.
  3. Rinse: rinse off all detergent and loosened debris under clean running water.
  4. Disinfect: apply food-safe disinfectant at the correct dilution, covering the whole surface, and leave it for the full contact time on the label, don't wipe it off early.
  5. Final rinse: rinse if the product label requires it.
  6. Dry: stand it upright to air-dry, rather than towel-drying with a cloth that could reintroduce bacteria.

Only once all six steps are done is the board actually safe to use for a different food type, not after step 2 or 3 because "it looks clean."

Air drying and safe chemical storage

Air drying is preferred over towel-drying because a cloth or towel, even one that looks clean, can carry bacteria from a previous use and simply reintroduce contamination onto a surface you've just spent five steps cleaning. Let equipment drain and air-dry wherever practical.

Safe chemical storage: keep cleaning chemicals in a dedicated area away from food and food-contact surfaces, always in their original, clearly labelled containers (never decanted into an unlabelled bottle or an old food/drink container), with COSHH data sheets available and staff trained on the specific products they're expected to use.

Cleaning schedules

A written cleaning schedule sets out what gets cleaned, how often, by whom, using what method/chemical, and how it's checked/recorded. This turns cleaning from an ad-hoc task into a controllable, auditable part of your food safety management system.

Quick check

The cleaning schedule shows a slicer was signed off as "cleaned" yesterday, but nobody can say what was actually done. Is that good enough? (click to reveal)

No. A schedule only works if what's recorded reflects what actually happened, method, product, and a genuine check, not just a signature out of habit. A tick with no real process behind it gives false confidence, not safety.

Signs of pest activity

  • Droppings, urine stains, or smear marks along walls/skirting.
  • Gnaw marks on packaging, wiring, or woodwork.
  • Damaged packaging, with or without visible gnawing.
  • Nesting material, shed insect skins, or a strong stale odour.
  • Live or dead pests, especially near food storage or waste areas.

What to do: droppings, gnawing, or damaged packaging

  1. Isolate the affected stock immediately: move it away from other food, don't leave it in place "for later."
  2. Don't use it. Any food in gnawed or damaged packaging, or anywhere near droppings, is contaminated and must not be sold or served, even if the food itself looks untouched.
  3. Report to your supervisor straight away, including exactly where and what you found.
  4. Dispose of the affected food following your premises' procedure, and clean and disinfect the area and any surfaces or equipment nearby.
  5. Record the sighting: date, location, what was found, and what was done with the stock. This record is also what a pest control contractor needs to spot a pattern.

Quick check

You find one small dropping near a storage shelf. It's just one, so do you need to do anything beyond sweeping it up? (click to reveal)

Yes. Isolate anything nearby that could be affected, don't use it, report it to your supervisor, clean and disinfect the area, and record what you found. One dropping is still a genuine sign of activity, not something to quietly clean away and forget.

What to do: a live rodent or a serious sighting

Seeing a live rodent during service is understandably alarming, but the food safety response is straightforward:

  1. Stay calm and don't try to catch or kill it yourself. That's not your job, and chasing it around a food area risks spreading contamination further.
  2. Isolate the area it was seen in: stop food handling there, and cover or move any exposed food nearby.
  3. Alert a supervisor immediately, who should contact your pest control contractor and consider whether the affected area needs to close temporarily.
  4. Don't resume normal work in that area until it's been checked, cleaned, and cleared.
  5. Record what was seen, when, and where, along with everything done afterward.

What to do: cockroaches and flies

Cockroaches: even a single sighting is significant, since they're usually a sign of an established, hidden infestation rather than a one-off. Treat it the same as a rodent sighting: isolate, report immediately, don't assume it was "just one."

Flies around food: cover or move exposed food immediately, check fly control measures (screens, electric fly killers) are working, and report persistent fly activity rather than treating it as background nuisance. Flies pick up bacteria from waste and drains and can deposit it directly onto food.

Both should be logged the same way as any other pest sighting, and reported to your pest control contractor if activity continues or repeats.

Preventing pests

  • Proofing: seal gaps around pipework, doors, and walls; fit door strips and screens.
  • Storage: keep food in sealed, pest-proof containers, off the floor.
  • Waste management: use lidded bins, empty frequently, keep bin storage areas clean and away from entrances.
  • Housekeeping: clear spills promptly; pests are drawn to food debris and standing water.

Summary

  • Cleaning removes dirt; disinfecting kills micro-organisms, and cleaning always comes first.
  • Contact time and correct dilution are what make disinfectant actually work; skipping either one means it doesn't.
  • Follow the six-stage cleaning process and a written schedule, and air-dry rather than towel-dry.
  • For any pest sign, big or small: isolate the affected food, don't use it, report immediately, clean the area, and record what happened.
  • Prevent pests through proofing, storage, and good waste management.
