Bacteriology & Food Poisoning

Duration: 13–17 minutes

What this module covers

  • What bacteria need to grow (FAT TOM) and how they actually multiply
  • The danger zone and high-risk foods
  • The main causes of food poisoning
  • Spores, heat-stable toxins, and why cooling speed matters
  • Why food can look and smell completely normal and still be dangerous
  • Viruses like norovirus
  • Incubation periods, symptoms, and vulnerable groups
  • What to do and report if you or a customer becomes unwell

What bacteria need to grow

Remember FAT TOM:

  • Food: a nutrient source (protein-rich foods especially).
  • Acidity: bacteria prefer near-neutral pH.
  • Time: given time, bacteria multiply exponentially.
  • Temperature: the 8–63°C "danger zone" is ideal for growth.
  • Oxygen: most food-poisoning bacteria need it (some don't).
  • Moisture: bacteria need water to survive and multiply.

How bacteria actually multiply

A single bacterium doesn't grow bigger, it splits in two. Each of those splits again, and so on. Under good conditions (the danger zone), that split can happen roughly every 10–20 minutes. That's not steady growth, it's exponential: 1 becomes 2, becomes 4, becomes 8, and so on.

Left at room temperature for a few hours, a small, harmless-sounding starting number of bacteria on a piece of food can multiply into the millions, easily enough to cause illness. This is exactly why "it was only out for a couple of hours" is not a safe assumption: time in the danger zone is the one factor that compounds fastest, and it's the one most within your control.

The danger zone

Between 8°C and 63°C, bacteria multiply fastest. Around 37°C (body temperature) is the optimum for most bacteria linked to food poisoning, which makes sense: those are the bacteria best adapted to surviving and thriving inside a human body afterwards.

This is why food shouldn't sit at room temperature for extended periods, and why chilling, cooking, and hot-holding temperatures matter so much (Module 5). Every rule in that module exists to either keep food out of this range, or to move it through this range as fast as possible.

High-risk foods

Ready-to-eat foods that support bacterial growth and won't be cooked again before eating: cooked meat and poultry, dairy, cooked rice, shellfish, eggs and egg products, gravies and stocks. These need the most careful temperature and cross-contamination control, because there's no final cooking step left to kill off anything that's gone wrong earlier.

Common causes of food poisoning

  • Salmonella: raw poultry, eggs; undercooking.
  • Campylobacter: the most common UK cause; raw/undercooked poultry, unpasteurised milk.
  • E. coli (incl. O157): raw/undercooked beef, unwashed produce; can be severe, especially in children.
  • Listeria: chilled ready-to-eat foods, soft cheeses; grows even in the fridge (slowly, but it doesn't stop); dangerous in pregnancy.
  • Staphylococcus aureus: spread by human skin/hands; produces a heat-stable toxin that reheating does not destroy.
  • Bacillus cereus: forms heat-resistant spores that survive cooking, especially in reheated/cooled rice; also produces a heat-stable toxin.
  • Clostridium perfringens: also spore-forming; large batches of meat/stews cooled too slowly let surviving spores germinate and multiply.

Spores and toxins: why cooling speed matters

Some bacteria (Bacillus cereus, Clostridium perfringens) form spores: a dormant, heat-resistant survival stage that can live through normal cooking. If cooked food is then cooled too slowly, surviving spores germinate and multiply in the danger zone. This is the actual mechanism behind the "cool within 90 minutes" rule in the next module, not just a general precaution.

Some bacteria also produce toxins that are heat-stable: once produced (e.g. by Staph. aureus or Bacillus cereus sitting too long in the danger zone), reheating the food to 75°C kills the bacteria but does not neutralise the toxin already in the food. Reheating cannot undo a food safety failure that already happened earlier in the process; it only kills what's currently alive, not what's already been produced and left behind.

Why food can look and smell completely normal and still be dangerous

This is one of the most important, and most misunderstood, ideas in food safety: the bacteria that make food dangerous are usually not the same bacteria that make food smell or taste "off." Spoilage bacteria produce the sour smells, sliminess, and off tastes people associate with bad food, but the infective dose of Salmonella, Campylobacter, or E. coli that causes real illness can be present in food that looks, smells, and tastes entirely normal.

The same is true of heat-stable toxins: they have no taste, smell, or visible sign at all. This is exactly why food safety relies on process, correct temperatures, correct times, correct handling, rather than on inspecting, smelling, or tasting food to judge whether it's safe. "It smells fine" is never a food safety test.

It's not just bacteria: viruses

Norovirus ("the winter vomiting bug") is one of the most common causes of food-borne illness in the UK. It's a virus, not a bacterium, spread through contaminated food, surfaces, and person-to-person contact. It takes only a very small amount to cause infection, and it spreads easily in kitchens and bars if hygiene lapses. This is the specific reason behind the 48-hour symptom-free exclusion rule covered in the next module: it stops a still-infectious person from handling food or touching shared surfaces.

Incubation periods: why the cause isn't always obvious

Incubation period is the gap between eating contaminated food and symptoms starting. It varies hugely by cause, which is exactly why a customer (or a member of staff) is often unable to say what made them ill:

  • A few hours: Staph. aureus and Bacillus cereus toxins act fast, since the toxin is already formed and doesn't need to multiply inside the body first.
  • 8–24 hours: Clostridium perfringens, Salmonella (can also take longer).
  • 2–5 days: Campylobacter, E. coli.
  • Up to several weeks: Listeria, which is part of why it's treated so seriously in pregnancy despite being rare.

A long incubation period means someone can feel completely fine leaving your premises and only become ill days later, with no obvious link back to a specific meal unless good records (Module 7) exist to help trace it.

Symptoms

Typical symptoms across most causes of food poisoning: nausea, vomiting, diarrhoea, stomach cramps, and fever. Symptoms can range from mild and short-lived to severe enough to need hospital treatment, particularly for E. coli, Listeria, and in vulnerable groups.

Vulnerable groups

Some customers are at much higher risk of severe illness: pregnant women, young children, the elderly, and anyone with a weakened immune system. Extra care with high-risk foods (unpasteurised products, undercooked eggs/meat, certain soft cheeses and pâtés) matters most for these groups, and is why some products carry specific warnings for pregnancy.

What to do and report

This module explains the mechanism; what you actually do with it in practice is:

  • If you have diarrhoea, vomiting, or symptoms that could be food-borne, follow the exclusion rules in Module 4, don't wait to "see how it goes" during a shift.
  • If a customer reports feeling unwell after eating with you, don't dismiss it or debate it with them. Take their details, note what they ate and when, and pass it straight to a supervisor.
  • Keep the batch, delivery, or preparation records (Module 7) that let a supervisor check what else might be affected. This is exactly why traceability records matter, not as paperwork for its own sake, but so a single report can be traced back to a specific delivery or prep batch.
  • Never assume a complaint is exaggerated because the food "looked and smelled fine." As covered on the earlier slide, that's not evidence either way.

Summary

  • Bacteria need food, the right acidity, time, warmth, oxygen and moisture (FAT TOM) to multiply, and they do it exponentially, not steadily.
  • 8–63°C is the danger zone; incubation periods range from hours to weeks depending on the cause.
  • Spores survive cooking and heat-stable toxins survive reheating, which is why badly handled food can't always be "fixed" by cooking it again.
  • Food that looks, smells, and tastes normal can still be dangerous. Process, not inspection, is what keeps food safe.
  • High-risk foods and vulnerable customers need the most careful handling, and any illness report needs to be recorded and escalated, not brushed off.
Quick self-check (click to reveal answer)

Q: A batch of cooked rice was left out too long, then reheated thoroughly before serving. A customer is later unwell. Why might reheating not have made it safe?

A: Bacillus cereus can produce a heat-stable toxin while the rice sat in the danger zone. Reheating kills the bacteria but doesn't destroy a toxin that's already been produced.
