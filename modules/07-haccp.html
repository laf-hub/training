What is HACCP?

Hazard Analysis and Critical Control Points (HACCP) is a systematic method for identifying, evaluating, and controlling food safety hazards, rather than relying on end-inspection alone. UK/EU law requires food businesses to have a documented food safety management system based on HACCP principles.

The 7 principles: overview

  1. Conduct a hazard analysis
  2. Identify Critical Control Points (CCPs)
  3. Establish critical limits
  4. Establish monitoring procedures
  5. Establish corrective actions
  6. Establish verification procedures
  7. Establish record-keeping

Hazard analysis & Critical Control Points

Hazard analysis: map out every step of a food's journey (delivery → storage → prep → cooking → holding → service) and identify where hazards could be introduced.

Critical Control Point (CCP): a step where control is essential to prevent, eliminate, or reduce a hazard to a safe level, e.g. cooking is almost always a CCP, since it's the step that kills harmful bacteria.

Critical limits & monitoring

Critical limit: the measurable value that separates safe from unsafe at a CCP, e.g. "core temperature must reach 75°C."

Monitoring: the planned checks that confirm the critical limit is being met, e.g. probing every batch of chicken and logging the reading.

Quick check

Why is "probe every batch and log it" a monitoring procedure, rather than just a good habit? (click to reveal)

Because it's the planned, systematic check that confirms a critical limit is actually being met, every time, not just occasionally. Without it, you'd only find out a CCP had failed after someone got ill, not before.

Corrective action, verification & records

Corrective action: what you do when monitoring shows a critical limit hasn't been met, e.g. return food to cooking until it reaches 75°C, or discard it.

Verification: periodic checks that the whole system is working as intended (e.g. management spot-checks, calibrating equipment).

Records: written evidence (temperature logs, cleaning schedules, delivery checks) that prove due diligence to an EHO.

Worked example: following a ready-to-eat cheese through the process

The seven principles can feel abstract until you follow one product through them from start to finish. Take a wheel of soft, ripened cheese, a high-risk, ready-to-eat product with no cooking step left before it's eaten, so every earlier stage has to be controlled properly on its own.

Step 1: receipt

Hazard: the cheese has been chilled throughout transport, but bacteria like Listeria can still grow, slowly, even in the fridge, if the cold chain has been broken.

CCP & critical limit: delivery temperature check, product must read at or below the agreed chilled limit (typically 8°C or below, ideally colder).

Monitoring: probe the delivery on arrival and log it, exactly as covered in Module 5. If it's too warm, that's a corrective action moment before the product ever reaches storage, not something to deal with later.

Step 2: storage

Hazard: temperature abuse in the fridge, or cross-contact with raw food or other allergens stored nearby.

CCP & critical limit: fridge temperature (0–5°C), correct shelf position, and use-by date control.

Monitoring: routine fridge temperature checks and stock rotation using FIFO (Module 9), so older stock is always used or sold first, and nothing sits past its use-by date because it was pushed to the back.

Quick check

Stock rotation has slipped and an older wheel of cheese has been pushed to the back, past its use-by date, while a newer one at the front got sold. What CCP failed here? (click to reveal)

The storage stage: use-by date control via FIFO stock rotation. The monitoring step, checking dates and rotating stock so older items are used first, wasn't followed, letting a high-risk ready-to-eat product go past its safety date unnoticed.

Step 3: preparation & cutting

Hazard: cross-contact from a knife or board used on a different cheese or allergen-containing product (Module 2), and time out of the fridge extending during a busy cutting session.

CCP & critical limit: dedicated or properly cleaned knife and board between products, and a maximum reasonable time out of chilled storage while cutting and portioning.

Monitoring: visually confirming the right equipment is used for the right product, and not letting a "quick" cutting job turn into cheese sitting out for an extended period while you're pulled onto something else.

Step 4: display & sale

Hazard: the display counter running warm, or cheese being left out for customer sampling longer than intended.

CCP & critical limit: chilled display temperature, and correct labelling (use-by date, full allergen information, batch/traceability reference, all covered in Modules 8 and 9).

Monitoring: periodic display temperature checks through the day, and confirming labels are correct and legible before anything goes on display.

Step 5: corrective action

Say the display counter is checked mid-afternoon and reads 11°C, above the target. This is where the whole system earns its keep:

  • Act immediately: move the cheese back to correct chilled storage while you investigate, and stop selling from that display until the fault is resolved.
  • Judge the product: using the same time/temperature reasoning as Module 5, decide whether it's still safe to sell, must be used differently, or must be discarded, based on how long it was actually out of temperature.
  • Fix the cause: check the unit, the door seal, or whether it was overloaded, so the same failure doesn't repeat tomorrow.
  • Record it: the reading, the time, the decision made about the product, and the fix. This is precisely the record that shows an EHO your system caught and controlled the problem, not that there wasn't a problem at all.

Safer Food, Better Business (SFBB)

Most small and medium food businesses in the UK don't need to write a HACCP plan completely from scratch. The FSA's free Safer Food, Better Business pack provides a ready-made, simplified food safety management system built on HACCP principles, with diary sheets for daily checks.

Summary

  • HACCP is a proactive, step-by-step system for controlling food safety hazards.
  • A Critical Control Point is a step where control is essential; cooking is the classic example, but so are delivery checks, storage, and display temperature for a ready-to-eat product like cheese.
  • Every stage a product passes through, receipt, storage, prep, display, sale, has its own hazard, control point, and monitoring check.
  • Records prove your system is actually being followed, day to day, not just that a problem never happened.

2. Why does HACCP require monitoring as well as just setting a critical limit? (click to reveal)

A limit on its own doesn't confirm anything. Monitoring is the planned, repeated check that proves the limit is actually being met in practice, so a failure is caught quickly rather than discovered after the fact.

3. A chilled cheese display is found running at 11°C. What's the correct first action? (click to reveal)

Move the product back to correct chilled storage and stop selling from that display immediately, then judge the product using time-out-of-temperature reasoning, fix the fault, and record what happened.

4. Cumulative: a chilled ready-to-eat product arrives warm. What temperature, contamination, traceability, and reporting checks apply? (click to reveal)

Temperature (Module 5): probe more than one item, check the core temperature, not just the surface. Contamination (Module 2): check packaging integrity, since a warm delivery may also mean a broken cold chain and higher spoilage risk. Traceability (Module 9): note the batch code before deciding what to do with it, so it can be tracked either way. Reporting: reject or escalate if in doubt, and record the reading, the decision, and the reason, not just "temperature was off."
