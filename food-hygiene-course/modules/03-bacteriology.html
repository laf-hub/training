Bacteriology & Food Poisoning

What bacteria need to grow

Remember FAT TOM:

  • Food — a nutrient source (protein-rich foods especially).
  • Acidity — bacteria prefer near-neutral pH.
  • Time — given time, bacteria multiply exponentially.
  • Temperature — the 8–63°C "danger zone" is ideal for growth.
  • Oxygen — most food-poisoning bacteria need it (some don't).
  • Moisture — bacteria need water to survive and multiply.

The danger zone

Between 8°C and 63°C, bacteria multiply fastest — some can double in number every 10–20 minutes. Around 37°C (body temperature) is the optimum for most bacteria linked to food poisoning.

This is why food shouldn't sit at room temperature for extended periods, and why chilling, cooking, and hot-holding temperatures matter so much (Module 5).

High-risk foods

Ready-to-eat foods that support bacterial growth and won't be cooked again before eating: cooked meat and poultry, dairy, cooked rice, shellfish, eggs and egg products, gravies and stocks. These need the most careful temperature and cross-contamination control.

Common causes of food poisoning

  • Salmonella — raw poultry, eggs; undercooking.
  • Campylobacter — the most common UK cause; raw/undercooked poultry, unpasteurised milk.
  • E. coli (incl. O157) — raw/undercooked beef, unwashed produce; can be severe, especially in children.
  • Listeria — chilled ready-to-eat foods, soft cheeses; grows even in the fridge; dangerous in pregnancy.
  • Staphylococcus aureus — spread by human skin/hands; produces a heat-resistant toxin.
  • Bacillus cereus — associated with reheated/cooled rice.
  • Clostridium perfringens — large batches of meat/stews cooled too slowly.

Symptoms & onset

Typical symptoms: nausea, vomiting, diarrhoea, stomach cramps, fever. Onset ranges from a few hours (e.g. Staph. aureus, Bacillus cereus toxins) to several days (e.g. Campylobacter, Listeria) after eating contaminated food — which is why outbreaks can be hard to trace back to a single meal.

Vulnerable groups

Some customers are at much higher risk of severe illness: pregnant women, young children, the elderly, and anyone with a weakened immune system. Extra care with high-risk foods (unpasteurised products, undercooked eggs/meat, certain soft cheeses and pâtés) matters most for these groups.

Summary

  • Bacteria need food, the right acidity, time, warmth, oxygen and moisture (FAT TOM) to multiply.
  • 8–63°C is the danger zone.
  • High-risk foods and vulnerable customers need the most careful handling.
